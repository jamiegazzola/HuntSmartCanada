How Does BC LEH Work?

British Columbia's Limited Entry Hunting system limits the number of hunters who can participate in certain hunts. Here is how applications, hunt codes, authorizations, and odds fit together — without the jargon.

HuntSmart CanadaPublished Oct 26, 2026Updated Oct 26, 20268 min read

Limited Entry Hunting (LEH) is used when British Columbia wants to limit hunting pressure for a particular species, area, season, or class of animal. Instead of every licensed hunter being able to hunt that opportunity, hunters apply to a draw and only successful applicants receive an authorization.

The simplest way to think about LEH: a hunt has a limited number of authorizations. Hunters apply for that hunt. The province conducts the draw. If you are successful, you can participate in that specific LEH opportunity subject to the current regulations.

1. Start with the hunt, not just the species

“Moose LEH” or “elk LEH” is not one single draw. Each opportunity is defined by a specific combination of species, management unit or area, season, animal class, and hunt code. That is why the provincial tables can become difficult to compare quickly.

Two moose hunts in the same region may have very different season dates, tag allocations, access, and historical demand. The hunt code identifies the exact opportunity you are applying for.

2. What is a hunt code?

A hunt code is the identifier attached to a specific LEH opportunity. When you apply, you are not simply applying for “Region 3 moose.” You are selecting a defined hunt from the current LEH synopsis.

HuntSmart organizes those opportunities into searchable cards so you can compare the attributes that matter before choosing a code.

3. What determines the odds?

At a high level, draw difficulty is driven by the relationship between demand and available authorizations. If a hunt attracts many more eligible applicants than there are available authorizations, it will be harder to draw.

For a simplified illustration, imagine a hunt with 500 comparable applications competing for 50 authorizations. That is a much more attainable opportunity than a hunt where 5,000 applications compete for the same 50 authorizations. The official draw process and application structure matter, so a simple permits-divided-by-applications calculation should be treated as context rather than a replacement for official draw rules.

4. Why historical odds matter

One year can be noisy. Applicant numbers change, authorizations change, hunters move between hunt codes, and regulation changes can shift demand. A hunt that looked unusually good one year may not remain that way.

That is why HuntSmart stores historical draw information by opportunity. The goal is not to pretend the future is perfectly predictable. It is to give you enough history to understand whether the current number is normal, improving, worsening, or unusually volatile.

Good LEH strategy is not “pick the highest percentage.” The best application for you balances draw probability with hunt quality, access, season timing, travel, and whether you can actually make the hunt count if you draw it.

5. Draw odds are only half the question

There are really two separate questions:

  1. Can I draw this hunt?
  2. If I draw it, is it an opportunity I actually want?

That second question can include harvest success, road or trail access, season dates, animal class, travel distance, terrain, and how much time you have available. A hunt with worse odds can still be the right application if the opportunity is substantially better for you.

6. A practical way to choose an LEH application

Start broad, then narrow:

  1. Choose the species you actually want to prioritize.
  2. Remove regions and management units you realistically cannot hunt.
  3. Check season dates against your schedule.
  4. Compare current and historical draw odds.
  5. Look at harvest and hunt context where available.
  6. Shortlist a few realistic opportunities instead of comparing the entire synopsis manually.

7. Where HuntSmart fits

The provincial synopsis remains the source you should use for current legal requirements. HuntSmart is the decision layer around that information: filtering opportunities, organizing historical draw data, comparing hunts, and making it easier to understand the trade-offs between them.

Instead of jumping between years of PDFs and spreadsheets, you can narrow the database to the species and areas that matter to you, then inspect the opportunity in context.

Common mistakes

  • Looking at only the latest year of draw odds.
  • Assuming the easiest draw is automatically the best hunt.
  • Ignoring season dates and access until after the application is submitted.
  • Comparing percentages without checking whether the underlying opportunity changed.
  • Using third-party information instead of the current official regulations for legal decisions.
